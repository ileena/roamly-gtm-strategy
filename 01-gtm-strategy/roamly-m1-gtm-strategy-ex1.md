# Roamly Groups · GTM Strategy, Module 1 (Exercise 1)

## Responses
- **What is the core business problem Roamly is trying to solve with Roamly Groups?:** Roamly is losing potential bookings because its current booking flow does not support group coordination. Travelers already want to book experiences together, but managing separate accounts, split payments, and availability creates friction and high drop-off. The core business problem is converting this existing group demand into completed bookings and revenue.
- **What does success look like if this launch goes well? Think beyond revenue, the product, the brand, and the customer relationship.:** Success means more groups complete their bookings with less coordination effort, turning existing demand into incremental bookings and revenue.

Beyond revenue, success includes:
- **Product:** Travelers successfully use shared planning and split payments, group-booking drop-off falls, and coordination-related support requests decrease.
- **Customer relationship:** Organizers and participants trust Roamly to handle group plans, report greater satisfaction, and return for future experiences.
- **Brand:** Roamly becomes known as a trusted choice for shared local experiences, extending its appeal beyond independent travelers.
- **Host network:** Hosts receive more group bookings they can reliably fulfill, while maintaining experience quality.

We would track group-booking completion, repeat bookings, customer satisfaction, referrals, and host cancellations against pre-launch baselines, while ensuring the solo-traveler experience remains strong.
- **What signals suggest this is a real market opportunity worth pursuing? What is the behavioral data telling you about unmet demand?:** The strongest signal is that users are already trying to book as groups despite Roamly’s limitations. They coordinate across separate accounts, split payments manually, and synchronize availability themselves. These workarounds show an existing need rather than hypothetical interest in a new feature.

High drop-off in group-booking sessions, alongside consistent support feedback, suggests that coordination friction is preventing users from completing bookings they want to make.

Roamly’s strong product ratings and repeat booking rates—three times the industry average—also indicate that its core experiences deliver value. Its network of 12,000 vetted hosts provides a foundation for serving group demand, although group capacity and readiness still need validation.

Together, these signals justify pursuing and testing the opportunity. We should quantify group demand and abandonment reasons, then test whether removing coordination friction increases completed bookings.
- **List your top 5 possible segments that could benefit from Roamly Groups.:** 1. **Friend groups traveling together:** Friends who want to discover local experiences together while coordinating availability and splitting payments across separate accounts.
2. **Families and multigenerational travel groups:** Relatives who need a shared itinerary and experiences that accommodate different ages, interests, and schedules.
3. **Corporate teams on retreats or offsites:** Team organizers who need to coordinate local activities for colleagues, confirm attendance, and manage a shared itinerary.
4. **Travel clubs and interest-based communities:** Groups formed around interests such as food, art, or adventure that want curated experiences and an easier way to coordinate participation and payments.
5. **Celebration groups:** People traveling for birthdays, reunions, or pre-wedding celebrations who need to organize a shared experience around a fixed date and coordinate payments among participants.

These are potential segments to validate, rather than confirmed customer groups. Friend groups are a strong initial hypothesis because their coordination and payment needs closely match the behaviors described in the scenario.
- **How might you size the market? Think industry reports, travel research, social listening, competitor analysis.:** I would combine external market research with Roamly’s own behavioral data to estimate the opportunity.

1. **Industry reports and travel research:** Use tourism-board data and travel-industry research to estimate spending on local experiences, the share of travelers taking group trips, average group size, and booking frequency. Focus on Roamly’s 40 cities rather than treating all global travel spending as the addressable market.

2. **Social listening:** Review travel communities, forums, and social media for recurring frustrations around group planning, payments, and availability. Use these insights to identify promising segments and unmet needs, rather than treating online mentions as a direct measure of market size.

3. **Competitor analysis:** Study platforms offering group experiences, comparing their target segments, destinations, pricing, group capacity, and booking flows. Where reliable data is available, use it to benchmark demand and identify underserved segments.

4. **Internal data and customer research:** Estimate how many Roamly booking attempts involve groups, where they drop off, and their potential booking value. Interview organizers and participants to validate demand, and check hosts’ capacity to accommodate groups.

5. **Build a bottom-up estimate:** Estimate annual eligible group-booking occasions × achievable booking share × average total group-booking value. This gives potential annual gross booking value; applying Roamly’s take rate estimates platform revenue.

I would distinguish the total relevant market (TAM), the portion Roamly can serve given its cities and host capabilities (SAM), and the portion it can realistically capture over a defined period (SOM). I would present low, base, and high scenarios and validate the most uncertain assumptions through a pilot.
- **Which cross-functional stakeholders would you include to validate and execute? Think beyond product and engineering, who has context on the customer, market, and commercial model?:** I would involve stakeholders early to help validate the opportunity and shape the strategy, then assign clear launch responsibilities.

- **Customer Support and User Research:** Identify recurring coordination problems, interview organizers and participants, and validate the main reasons for group-booking abandonment.
- **Data and Analytics:** Size existing group demand, establish baseline conversion and repeat-booking rates, and design experiments to measure incremental bookings.
- **Product Marketing and Growth:** Validate priority segments, positioning, messaging, and acquisition channels, then execute targeted launch campaigns and onboarding.
- **Host Operations and Partnerships:** Confirm hosts’ group capacity, availability, pricing, and service readiness. Explore relevant distribution partners where evidence supports them.
- **Finance and Payments:** Validate unit economics, payment-processing costs, and the commercial model. Define how split payments, refunds, and host payouts will work.
- **Legal, Privacy, and Trust & Safety:** Review shared-itinerary permissions, payment and cancellation terms, and responsibilities when one participant cancels or fails to pay.
- **Sales or Business Development, if targeting organizations:** Validate corporate or travel-club buying needs, including invoicing and procurement requirements.

Product would coordinate the work with Design and Engineering, with a named owner and readiness criteria for each launch workstream.
- **Which one segment is the most valuable to target first, and why?:** I would prioritize small groups of friends planning a trip together, starting with groups organized by existing Roamly users.

Their needs closely match the observed friction: coordinating across separate accounts, splitting payments, and aligning availability. Roamly Groups directly addresses these tasks.

This segment also builds on Roamly’s existing independent-traveler audience and discovery channels, making it a practical starting point for acquisition and testing. Existing users can invite friends into a shared booking, potentially introducing new customers to the platform.

Compared with corporate groups, friend groups are less likely to require additional sales processes, procurement, or invoicing capabilities, allowing a more focused initial launch.

This is the strongest initial hypothesis, rather than a proven highest-value segment. I would validate it through group-booking completion, incremental revenue, acquisition cost, repeat bookings, and host capacity.
